# Cosmos Hub mainnet app-hash event: v28.1.0 emergency patch

## Incident report

**Chain:** Cosmos Hub mainnet (`cosmoshub-4`) → **Date:** September 5, 2026 → **Patch version:** `v28.1.0`

## Summary

On September 5, 2026, Cosmos Hub mainnet halted following an app-hash divergence event, addressed by an emergency security patch (`v28.1.0`) that, unlike a standard Cosmos Hub upgrade, was not published as a public release or put through on-chain governance. Because the distribution method fell so far outside normal Cosmos Hub protocol, we treated it as unverified until we could confirm it independently, and only applied it to our validator infrastructure once that confirmation was in hand. The upgrade went through cleanly, our validator did not lose any uptime at any point during the incident, and our nodes have been stable since.

This report exists to document why we deviated from our usual rule of only running what's public and governance-approved, and exactly how we satisfied ourselves that this specific deviation was justified. It's for our own record, and for other operators who may face the same judgment call.

## Why this fell outside normal protocol

A consensus-breaking change on Cosmos Hub is normally expected to ship as a public release on `cosmos/gaia`, referenced by an on-chain governance proposal, verifiable by anyone. `v28.1.0` had none of that: no GitHub release (we checked, it returned a 404), no proposal, no forum or public advisory at the time. What we had instead was a binary offered through a private channel, accompanied by a checksum from that same source, which confirms the download wasn't corrupted in transit but says nothing about who actually built it or whether the source was legitimate.

Taken at face value, this is indistinguishable from a targeted social-engineering attempt against validator operators, a pattern that does happen in this ecosystem, and one where a chain-halt is exactly the kind of urgency an attacker would want to exploit.

## Timeline (UTC)

- **~12:08, Sep 5** — First reports surface in validator community channels that mainnet has halted. Several operators mention a `v28.1.0` upgrade already applied on their end.
- **12:08 to 12:34, Sep 5** — Confusion spreads: the latest version publicly visible in `cosmos/gaia` is still `v28.0.0`. A validator confirms `v28.1.0` is a security patch distributed as a closed-source binary directly to a subset of validators, with no public source available.
- **~12:42, Sep 5** — Our own infrastructure confirms the halt: block height frozen, no further blocks produced.
- **Afternoon, Sep 5** — A public post in a Cosmos Hub community channel states that over 67% of consensus power has already upgraded to `v28.1.0`, warns that validators who don't upgrade risk being left behind, and points non-upgraded operators to a direct contact for instructions.
- **Afternoon/evening, Sep 5** — We receive the upgrade instructions privately, through that contact, with a request to keep them within our own team, and no way to verify the source independently through the channel itself.
- **Sep 5, same day** — We seek and obtain corroboration from a second, independent contact within the Cosmos Hub team before taking any action on signing infrastructure.
- **Sep 5, same day** — With that second confirmation in hand, we apply `v28.1.0` to our validator nodes, one at a time. Both resync and resume normal block production without incident and without any loss of validator uptime.
- **Sep 9, 2026** — The Cosmos Hub validator announcements channel publishes the first official, public reference to this event, confirming it publicly for the first time (see below).

## How we justified proceeding

We didn't proceed on trust alone. Before applying anything to a node with access to validator keys, we required all of the following to hold:

1. **The halt itself was independently verifiable.** We confirmed via our own infrastructure, not via the community reports, that mainnet had actually stopped producing blocks, and that it stayed stopped for hours. This ruled out a false alarm designed purely to manufacture urgency.
2. **Corroboration through a second, independent channel.** A single contact, even one with a verified role and prior relationship, was not treated as sufficient for a change touching consensus and signing keys. We obtained confirmation from a second, separately-reachable person on the Cosmos Hub team before proceeding.
3. **No irreversible action until both of the above were satisfied.** We staged the binary and verified its checksum in advance, but did not move it into place or restart any signing process until the second confirmation came through. This let us move quickly once confirmed, without ever having exposed signing infrastructure to an unverified binary.
4. **A rollback path was ready regardless.** We kept the previous binary in place as an instant revert, and rolled the update out to one node before the other, rather than both at once.

## Resolution

Both validator nodes were upgraded to `v28.1.0`, resynced, and resumed normal block production without incident. No slashing or missed-block penalties were incurred, and the validator maintained continuous uptime throughout the entire event.

## Retroactive confirmation (September 9, 2026)

Four days after the event, the official Cosmos Hub validator announcements channel posted the first public acknowledgment:

> If your Cosmos Hub validator was jailed following the recent app-hash event, you'll need to upgrade to v28.1.0, resync your node, and submit an unjail transaction once you're caught up. v28.1.0 isn't published as a public release. Please email nodes@cosmoslabs.io and we'll get you the upgrade instructions. Please send us an email from your organization's team email address. We are not currently providing upgrade instructions to providers messaging from personal email accounts, as we are unable to verify the sender's identity.

Two things stand out here. First, it confirms `v28.1.0` was a legitimate response to a real app-hash divergence, and that it remains deliberately unpublished, consistent with the closed distribution we saw on September 5, not a sign that it was fabricated. Second, the official channel is now applying the same principle we applied on our own four days earlier: don't take a single unverifiable contact at face value for something this sensitive. Verify identity through a channel that can't be trivially spoofed (in their case, an organizational email domain; in ours, a second independent human contact) before handing out instructions that touch consensus.

## Lessons learned

- **Preparation and verification aren't mutually exclusive.** Downloading and checksumming a binary in advance cost us nothing and meant we lost no time once the patch was confirmed. The risk lives in *executing* unverified code, not in having it staged.
- **A single "trusted" contact isn't enough for consensus-critical changes.** Verified identity on one platform, even with an established relationship and the right role, doesn't rule out a compromised account. Requiring corroboration through a second, independent channel before touching signing infrastructure was worth the extra time, even under halt pressure, and turned out to mirror what the chain team itself now requires.
- **Closed-source, out-of-band patches put every operator in the same bind.** Even when legitimate, a patch that can't be checked against a public source or a governance record forces each validator to make an individual trust judgment, under time pressure, with real stake on the line. We'd encourage chain teams handling this kind of emergency to publish at minimum a signed advisory or governance-adjacent record as early as possible, even if the code itself stays embargoed briefly for security reasons.

## For other validators

If you're in the same position, an urgent, privately-distributed "security patch" with no public source, our recommendation is: verify through more than one independent channel before running anything on signing infrastructure, stage the binary without executing it while you wait, and keep a fast rollback path ready regardless of how confident you are in the source.
